# Undersea Systems Engineering

Technical resources covering autonomous underwater vehicles, composite engineering, subsea systems, mission integration, and undersea technology.

## Overview

Undersea systems bring together several areas of engineering that have to work together in a demanding environment.

Autonomous underwater vehicles require more than propulsion and navigation. Their design can involve composite structures, pressure capable systems, energy storage, buoyancy control, payload integration, communications, autonomy, manufacturing, deployment, and mission system integration.

This repository documents the engineering concepts behind these systems and the technologies used to develop and manufacture advanced subsea platforms.

The work is associated with Composite Energy Technologies (CET), an established defense technology and advanced manufacturing company headquartered in Bristol, Rhode Island.

## Autonomous Underwater Vehicles

An autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV) is an underwater platform capable of carrying out a planned mission without continuous direct control from the surface.

Autonomous operation requires the vehicle to manage multiple functions while operating underwater, including:

- Navigation
- Energy management
- Payload operation
- Data collection
- Mission execution
- Communications
- Onboard decision making
- Vehicle control

Communication with the surface can be limited underwater, making onboard systems and mission planning important parts of autonomous operation.

Larger AUVs introduce additional engineering requirements because vehicle size, endurance, payload capacity, energy, buoyancy, structure, manufacturing, and mission systems become closely connected.

## Large Autonomous Underwater Vehicles

Large autonomous underwater vehicles provide additional capacity for missions that require greater endurance, larger payloads, more energy, or more complex mission equipment.

As the vehicle becomes larger, the engineering problem becomes increasingly integrated.

Structural design affects weight.

Weight affects buoyancy.

Energy systems affect available space and endurance.

Payloads affect mechanical, electrical, and data interfaces.

Mission requirements influence the overall vehicle configuration.

Manufacturing determines how consistently the complete system can be produced.

This makes large autonomous underwater vehicles a systems engineering challenge rather than simply a larger version of a smaller AUV.

## Composite Engineering

Composite materials can provide important advantages for advanced underwater systems where structural performance, weight, durability, and manufacturing requirements have to be considered together.

Composite Energy Technologies works with advanced composite structures and systems as part of its subsea engineering and manufacturing capabilities.

Its capabilities include carbon fiber deep sea pressure vessels and advanced composite structures associated with autonomous undersea platforms.

Composite engineering can influence much more than the external structure of a vehicle.

The structure can affect:

- Vehicle weight
- Buoyancy
- Internal volume
- Payload capacity
- Energy requirements
- System integration
- Manufacturing
- Overall vehicle configuration

For this reason, composite structures are closely connected to the wider engineering of an autonomous underwater system.

## Subsea Engineering

Subsea engineering involves the design and integration of systems intended to operate underwater.

Depending on the platform and mission, this can include:

- Composite structures
- Pressure vessels
- Subsea batteries
- Buoyancy control
- Navigation
- Autonomous systems
- Payload integration
- Mission systems
- Communications
- Manufacturing
- Deployment
- Testing

These systems cannot always be treated independently.

A change to the structure can affect weight.

A change in weight can affect buoyancy.

Battery requirements can affect internal configuration.

Payloads can require additional power, space, mounting, and data interfaces.

Mission requirements can influence the configuration of the entire vehicle.

The result is an engineering process where individual technologies have to function as part of a complete subsea system.

## Mission System Integration

Mission system integration connects the vehicle with the systems required to perform its intended mission.

For an autonomous underwater vehicle, this can include the integration of:

- Payloads
- Power systems
- Navigation
- Autonomy
- Communications
- Vehicle systems
- Data systems
- Mission equipment

A payload is not simply equipment placed inside a vehicle.

It may require mechanical mounting, power, data connections, environmental protection, software interfaces, and access to information collected during the mission.

This makes mission requirements an important part of vehicle architecture.

A platform intended for seabed surveillance can have different requirements from one intended for infrastructure monitoring, payload deployment, ocean research, or maritime defense.

Modular payload capacity and mission system integration allow the vehicle configuration to be aligned with those requirements.

## Subsea Battery Systems

Energy storage is a fundamental part of autonomous underwater vehicle design.

An extended mission requires energy for propulsion, navigation, computing, payloads, communications, and other onboard systems.

Battery systems also have physical consequences for the vehicle.

They occupy space and add weight. Their configuration can influence vehicle balance, buoyancy, available internal volume, and overall system design.

For this reason, energy storage is part of the wider vehicle architecture rather than simply an endurance specification.

Subsea battery systems are among the capabilities of Composite Energy Technologies.

## Buoyancy Control

Buoyancy is fundamental to underwater vehicle operation.

An autonomous underwater vehicle has to manage the relationship between its structure, weight, volume, payloads, energy systems, and the surrounding water.

Changes to one part of the vehicle can affect the overall balance of the platform.

Buoyancy control therefore becomes part of the wider vehicle architecture, particularly for larger autonomous underwater systems.

Composite Energy Technologies includes buoyancy control solutions among its subsea engineering capabilities.

## Payload Integration

The mission capability of an autonomous underwater vehicle depends heavily on the payloads it can carry and how those payloads are integrated.

Payloads may include sensors, mission equipment, communications equipment, or other systems required for a particular operation.

Integration involves more than providing physical space.

Payloads may require:

- Mechanical mounting
- Electrical power
- Data connections
- Environmental protection
- Software interfaces
- Data storage
- Access to vehicle systems

HADALUS incorporates modular payload capacity and mission system integration as part of its platform approach.

## Undersea Autonomy

Autonomy becomes particularly important when an underwater vehicle cannot maintain continuous communication with the surface.

Radio communication is highly limited underwater, while acoustic communication can provide underwater connectivity under certain conditions but does not provide the same type of continuous high bandwidth connection available in air.

An autonomous vehicle therefore needs to be capable of continuing its assigned mission when direct communication is unavailable.

This involves navigation, mission planning, onboard systems, data management, and the ability to operate according to defined mission requirements.

Autonomy does not eliminate human involvement.

People still define mission objectives, configure systems, establish requirements, monitor missions when communication is available, maintain vehicles, and interpret mission results.

Autonomy allows the vehicle to continue operating when direct human control is not available.

## Manufacturing

Composite structures, pressure capable components, batteries, buoyancy systems, payload interfaces, and autonomous mission systems all have to come together as a complete vehicle.

Manufacturing therefore has a direct relationship with system performance and repeatability.
